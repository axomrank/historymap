# Medieval Assam: migration, formation, expansion and reversals

Researched and implemented 4 November 2026. Scope: Sukaphaa’s Assam journey and early Ahom state formation, Chutia evidence and later wars, Kamata/Khen stages, and separate Bhuyan chiefdoms. This revises the earlier atlas polygons; it does not claim exact medieval cadastral boundaries.

## What changed

- Rivers have a 14-screen-pixel hit target, hover/focus names and a persistent name on tap. Highlight and tooltip clear on another map tap, Escape, a date change or dragging. Names refer to the mapped modern course, not proof of its medieval alignment.
- Ahom migration uses cumulative locality symbols until 1253, followed by a small reconstructed core. Charaideo is no longer a capital from 1228. Dashed connectors show sequence and direction, not a recorded GPS path. A focus control enlarges the region.
- Chutia has an early Sadiya locator, late-14th-century evidence, western grant localities from 1392, and a broader reconstructed domain context from the mid-15th century. These evidence dates are explicitly NOT conquest dates. The traditional Birpal/1187 date remains qualified.
- Kamata begins as a reorganisation of existing western authority, not a state growing out of an empty point. Contested eastern reach and subsequent fragmentation are separate from the core. Early, intermediate and later Khen envelopes distinguish Niladhwaj’s base from Nilambar’s larger domain.
- Bhuyans use separated locality patches and stages of attestation, resistance and subordination. They are not one unified kingdom, a single caste or always exactly twelve chiefs. Different lineages and chronological traditions must not be fused.

## Ahom route and chronology

The principal route source directly checked is Gait, *A History of Assam* (1906), pp.74–76 (printed pagination), a public-domain synthesis of Buranjis. It reports Patkai/Khamjang and Nongnyang before Namrup; the Sessa crossing, upstream Dihing exploration and return to Tipam; Mungklang Chekhru/Abhaypur in 1236; Habung in 1240; downstream travel to the Dikhou mouth and Ligirigaon in 1244; Simaluguri in 1246; and Charaideo in 1253. The upstream trip is drawn as a schematic two-way excursion. Modern channels are only a geographic aid.

Pangsau is a conventional pass identification. This work has not established the exact medieval crossing trail. The original version began at the Patkai corridor. The subsequent migration extension described below adds a deliberately schematic Yunnan–Myanmar itinerary with an expanded eastern frame. Abhaypur’s old site is unresolved here and is deliberately unpinned. Ligirigaon and Simaluguri use approximate modern vicinities. Habung uses the Dhakuakhana-region identification; the historical tract was larger and discussions also associate it with Dhemaji. Reported stops are distinguished from confidence in their modern coordinates.

1253 follows the conventional chronology in Gauhati University teaching material; UNESCO independently confirms Charaideo’s role as the first capital, not every intermediate year. Variants 1251–1252 occur in historical literature. The 1230 Tipam stage is a playback anchor for the interval before 1236. Agricultural settlement, force, intermarriage and accommodation all belong to state formation; the route is not equivalent to uniform annexation of every place crossed.

Suteuphaa’s Dikhou–Namdang acquisition is associated with his 1268–1281 reign. 1268 is a display-window start, not a known campaign date. IGNOU’s Unit 11 describes the Namdang limit and the following long consolidation. No fictitious annual expansion is interpolated through those two centuries.

Gait pp.84–90 supplies the war sequence: Habung annexed 1512; Chutia attack and Ahom gains 1513 (some accounts 1516); Chutia recovery at Mungkhrang 1520; Ahom recovery and further advance 1522; decisive Chutia annexation 1523, often dated 1523–24 in later scholarship; and subsequent western campaigns, ending with Dimapur’s sack in 1536. Two Namdang contexts must not be conflated: the Sivasagar tributary and the eastern wartime locality. Mungkhrang, Sessa/Tiphao forts and changing river mouths cannot be pinned exactly with the evidence consulted. They use regional patches. Wartime Habung control in 1520–22 remains unresolved, shown with hatching rather than a definite reconquest.

## Chutia: evidence dates versus territorial dates

Nandin/Nandisvara belongs to the late-14th-century genealogy recorded by Satyanarayana. The 1392 Dhenukhana and 1401 Ghilamara grants are independently referenced in NBU epigraphic research. Neog’s 1977 article reconstructs related rulers from the inscriptions; this session verified its bibliographic record and secondary quotations, but did not obtain the full article. The 1428 Sadiya–Chepakhowa charter is therefore explicitly an indirectly checked item. Its Habung official and proposed Kamatapura genealogy should not become a claim of Chutia rule across all Assam or all Kamata.

The Arunachal Pradesh State Gazetteer independently describes the Paya–Tamreswari enclosure inscription of Mukta-dharmanarayana, Saka1364/1442. It is evidence of royal patronage at a temple, not an expansion event. The map points for Tamreswari and inscription regions are approximate locators, not verified original issue or find coordinates. No claim of a pan-Arunachal hill empire follows from them.

The broader 1442–1511 envelope combines the preceding grants and the temple inscription with a reconstructed plains-domain interpretation. It neither proves conquest in 1442 nor uninterrupted occupation. Before this point, separate Sadiya, Habung and Ghilamara locality symbols avoid joining the intervening country merely from a grant. The source record does not support a smooth annual conquest animation. A date slider that pretends otherwise would be less accurate.

The extinction of independent monarchy in 1523–24 is distinct from the continued existence of Chutia people, Deori ritual traditions, local resistance and the incorporation of specialists into Ahom institutions. Last-king names and death narratives differ by chronicle; the unverified traditional royal reign list is suppressed in the interface.

## Kamata and the Bhuyans

The NBU chapter, printed pp.57–58, distinguishes rival western rulers, uncertain Arimatta traditions and outlying Bhuyan authority. Gait pp.36–43 also describes independent chiefs, temporary alliances, divergent origin traditions, changing authority east/west of Sankosh, the small early Khen base and Nilambar’s later expansion. Hence an inherited western Kamata focus, uncertain eastern reach, fragmentation and later Khen expansion are shown as separate stages. c.1300,1365,1440,1460 and1480 are approximate reconstruction/reign anchors, not exact border-change dates. Intermediate polygon edges are illustrative envelopes, not newly discovered frontiers.

The 1449 Bardowa anchor marks Sankardeva’s birth into a Bhuyan family, independently reported in the Census of India Nagaon handbook. It is NOT a conquest date or proof that settlement began then. Langamaguri/Lengamaguri near Barnadi is a traditional regional association, not a precisely located town. North-bank chiefdom symbols are illustrative localities, not mapped estates of named chiefs. The 1505 western recovery and 1515 Koch-pressure anchors represent processes with uncertain local timing. Subordination in the 1530s did not extinguish all families or offices. Official Nagaon district history records the later reorganisation of Bhuyan legacies under Ahom officers.

## Geometry and verification

The locality patches have intentionally symbolic radii; neither their areas nor the thin timeline traces measure possessions. Broad low-confidence envelopes have dashed borders. Campaign/contested hatching does not subtract from settled territories or count as core area. Existing later Ahom stages after1536 remain in place. Modern river lines are geographic references, not assertions that 13th-century waterways followed exactly those courses. Pitha labels and red boundary rivers remain restricted to the earlier, pre1100 interpretation.

The route data records latitude/longitude before projection. Research stages, map symbols, source access limitations and uncertainty are stored in the JSON; a companion CSV lists every stage and its source IDs. Automated checks cover early settlement size, delayed Charaideo capital, later growth, the1513/1520/1522 reversal, separate Habung acquisition, changing Khen stages, disjoint Bhuyan patches, valid sources and all annual map joins. Browser checks cover named river interaction and representative stage navigation.

## Added: Mong Mao–Myanmar migration corridor, 1215–1228

The later request explicitly asks for an imaginary winding line. A gold dashed corridor now connects the Mong Mao regional locator near Ruili (24.018N,97.856E), western Yunnan, the upper Irrawaddy / Waingmaw–Myitkyina vicinity, Mong Kwang / Mogaung, Hukawng, Nong Yang / Khamjang and Pangsau. It joins the existing red Patkai–Namrup route at the same pass coordinate. The eastern atlas frame extends to 98.7°E so Mong Mao is not clipped. The full-journey focus button fits the entire trail; the Assam focus remains available for the later settlements.

The line has 34 hand-placed vertices. The intermediate bends are illustrative cartography, not evidence of actual zigzags, exact footpaths, specific river crossings or particular camps. It is deliberately previewed in full in the 1215–1227 context rather than assigning fabricated arrival years to Myanmar stops. Only the conventional departure 1215 and Assam arrival 1228 are date anchors. Intermediate point labels say their dates are unknown. The migration party is not depicted as owning a Myanmar kingdom, and the Ahom polity still starts in 1228.

The *Comprehensive History of Assam*, vol.II, Tai-Ahom migration chapter (2003 edition), gives a sequence through old muangs to Wieng Maw opposite Myitkyina, a crossing to the western Irrawaddy bank, Soi-Ke, Mogaung and Nong Yang. The searchable book excerpt was checked; full-volume access was unavailable. This supports the Waingmaw/Mogaung ordering without locating every old muang. The many unlocated old place names have not received false exact pins. The SITA Assam report, p.191, independently supports the larger Mong Mao–Hukawng–Patkai–Mogaung corridor; its indexed text was checked, but its direct download now returns 404. Nath and Tripathi (2024), *Ancient Asia*15 p.84, also mention 1215 and passage through Mong-Kwang/Patkai; this open article was read, but it is not a detailed route survey.

An indexed *Indian Journal of Tai Studies*2018 account instead suggests Wing Men may be Bhamo. This differs from the Wieng Maw/Myitkyina identification. Bhamo is therefore not added as a certain stop or forced into the same itinerary. Other Tai chronicle traditions associate departure with Mong Mit in 1227. The display adopts the user’s requested conventional Mong Mao/1215 narrative and records these alternatives as uncertainty. No unsupported year is assigned to Mogaung, Hukawng or the Irrawaddy crossing.

Modern coordinates are only reference locations. Mong Mao is a regional Shweli/Ruili locator, not a verified palace point. Mogaung and Waingmaw use modern town vicinities; Hukawng is a broad valley symbol. Nong Yang uses the conventional modern lake identification near Pangsau. The route does not borrow the precise alignment of the WWII Stilwell Road and does not equate a modern road with a 13th-century migration track.

The source register records direct versus indexed access. [Route vertices CSV](migration-route-points.csv) lists all illustrative vertices. The stage CSV includes the new 1215–1227 context. Automated checks ensure the endpoints join, the entire route lies within the expanded frame, the new context joins the 1228 stage, and no pre 1228 Ahom territory is introduced.


## Geography and pacing revision

The full-route preview has been replaced by progressive distance-based playback over 1215–1228. This is illustrative pacing, not a dated itinerary or an assertion of arrival at every intermediate place in a particular year. Unreached Myanmar reference pins are hidden before 1228. The land corridor is softly curved for readability; river sections remain unsmoothed and share the base river's exact stored vertices. Disconnected modern river traces are drawn as separate sections, leaving gaps rather than inventing straight confluence links. The map's river geometry remains a modern reference, not a reconstruction of medieval channels.

At the user's request, Chutia and Ahom footprints east of 94.2E are constrained to the current Assam outline, used as an adopted plain–hill convention. This is not an assertion that the modern state boundary exactly traces every hill foot or historical frontier. Western acquisitions remain independent of this eastern clipping rule. River-side territorial masks use actual stored river polylines; compound historical limits retain their existing uncertain envelopes. Broad hand-drawn polygons and symbolic patches remain explicitly approximate; decorative waviness has not been added to historical borders. Existing ridge data provides geographic context, not a measured elevation model. The map is larger, with collapsible details and timeline controls.
